Central Event

Iran and Oman reach agreement on sharing of waters and revenues in the Strait of Hormuz

The Iranian Revolutionary Guard announced, in a report corroborated by multiple sources, that Tehran and Muscat reached an understanding on the delimitation of their respective waters in the Strait of Hormuz and on the sharing of revenues generated by navigation fees in the passage. The agreement emerges alongside a report of a US proposal — supposedly conveyed by the Pakistani army chief in talks in Tehran — offering sanctions relief in exchange for the full restoration of navigation and the suspension of attacks by Iran-aligned groups. The set signals a move toward the institutionalization of Iranian control over the chokepoint through which 20-30% of global oil transits, converting military leverage into economic and diplomatic bargaining. The counterpoint comes from Israel: Netanyahu declared a diplomatic agreement with Iran to be impossible, and the Iranian military warned that any future war could hit regional energy infrastructure — keeping tail risk alive.

Energy Impact

The prospect of an orderly reopening of Hormuz and of revenue sharing reduces the risk premium embedded in Brent and eases the immediate threat to the physical flow from the Gulf. Gold's retreat from its peak and WTI's intraday decline are consistent with a partial unwinding of the geopolitical hedge. However, Israel's rejection of any understanding with Tehran and the Iranian warning about energy infrastructure targets impose a floor on the premium, preventing full normalization in this window.

The structural interest at stake is the stability of fuel import parity, which governs Petrobras's pass-through and inflationary pressure, at a time when the FX rate around R$ 5.15 amplifies any international price shock.

Iran-Oman agreement on sharing of waters and Hormuz revenues signals de-escalation at the chokepoint

Diesel import parity, which is documented to respond to movements in the Strait of Hormuz, OPEC, and the sanctions regime, tends to ease as the risk premium in Brent compresses with the institutionalization of Iranian control over the passage. A Brent in moderate downside bias narrows the differential between the domestic price and international parity, reducing pressure on Petrobras for adjustments and marginally easing the fuel component in the IPCA (Brazilian CPI). Structurally, this reduces the tension between the state company's pricing policy and the fiscal cost of any subsidy, but Brazil's dependence on international pricing remains intact — the relief is cyclical, not structural, and reversible at the first naval incident.
